The applicant had a great screenshot, wrote perfect English and promised to cover nights. Five days in, he missed a shift, called a fan by another creator's pet name and sent your best buyer a set he already owned.

01 / Sourcing

Where people find Fanvue chatters

Telegram and Discord chatter groups are the fastest. Post in the evening and you'll have applicants by morning. They're also where experience is hardest to verify, and people who arrive fast can leave fast.

Reddit has communities where chatters look for work. Read each community's rules before posting, as some don't allow hiring posts.

Job boards and freelance marketplaces give you a bigger pool and payment handling, but some restrict adult-industry work. Check the terms before you post.

Referrals from creators you trust are the best quality and the smallest pool.

What to put in the job post

Hours and time zone you need covered. Languages your fans write in. Pay structure. Fanvue experience, and whether the account is an AI model. A paid test chat as the first step. And one line that saves you hours: “Access is through Fanvue team-member accounts. I will never share a password.”

02 / Screening

Run a paid test chat before anyone touches your fans

Screenshots of big months tell you about an account, not the person. A test chat tells you how they think. Pay for an hour of it, play the fan yourself, and build the scenarios from things that actually happen in your inbox.

Four test scenarios and what to look for
ScenarioGood signRed flag
“Can I get it cheaper?”Holds the price, offers a smaller item or keeps chattingHalves the price straight away
A request outside your boundariesSays no warmly and steers somewhere elseAgrees to anything to close the sale

That last one isn't optional. Fanvue's AI content rules say a fully AI account must never tell fans it's a real person. A chatter who'd bend that rule to make a sale is a risk to the account.

Read their replies for voice, too. Do they sound like your persona, or like a script? Do they pick up on what the “fan” said, or answer a different question?

03 / Warning signs

Red flags that show up in the first week

  • Ghosting during the trial. If they vanish for a day when it's a test, they'll vanish on a live Saturday night.
  • Late to shifts. Ten minutes late every day is fans waiting every day.
  • Persona slips. Wrong city, wrong backstory, a pet name from another account they work on.
  • Copy-paste openers. The same line to every fan, word for word.
  • Discounting to close. Prices that don't match the ladder you gave them.
  • Asking for your password. They don't need it. Fanvue's team-member access exists for this.
  • Moving fans off Fanvue. “Add me on Telegram” or side payments. Fanvue's compliance guidance says to keep conversations and payments on the platform.

One of these is a conversation. Two in the first week is usually your answer.

04 / The brief

What to put in a Fanvue persona brief

A chatter can only be as consistent as what you've written down. Before the first shift, give them:

  1. Persona facts and disclosure

    Name, city, age range, backstory, and on AI-model accounts the disclosure wording that must stay intact.

  2. Voice examples

    Five or six real messages in the voice you want, plus words and habits to avoid.

  3. Boundaries

    What the persona will never do or send, and how to say no.

  4. Content and prices

    What's available, the price ladder, and the discount rules. Our Fanvue PPV pricing guide shows how to build one.

  5. Approvals

    Who approves customs and exceptions, and how to reach them during a shift.

  6. Shift notes

    Where notes live and what a good handover note says: what happened, what's open, what to do next.

Our Fanvue persona brief guide has a template you can copy and fill in.

05 / Week one

Set up access and read the first week

Add the chatter as a team member under a Fanvue manager account, not with your login. Our checklist for adding a chatter to Fanvue goes step by step, including the default that lets team members see every creator under the same manager.

Then read their conversations. All of them, for the first few shifts. Check the gaps between a fan's message and the reply, whether prices match the ladder, whether anything was offered twice, and whether promises were kept. It's tedious. It's also the only way to know before your fans tell you by going quiet.

Pay structure shapes behaviour. Hourly-only pay can reward being online over selling, and commission-only pay can reward pushing too hard. Our guide to what Fanvue chatters cost walks through the trade-offs with a worked example.

07 / Before you go

Common questions

How many chatters do I need to cover Fanvue 24/7?

It depends on how many conversations your account gets. One person can't cover 24 hours, so full coverage means at least three shifts a day plus cover for days off and no-shows. Size it by inbox volume, not by a fixed ratio.

Should I pay Fanvue chatters hourly or by commission?

Each rewards something different. Hourly pay is predictable but pays for time online; commission pays for sales but can encourage hard selling. Some teams mix the two. Our Fanvue chatter cost guide has a worked example.

Can I hire chatters for an AI-model Fanvue account?

Yes. Give them the persona brief and the disclosure rules in writing, and test how they answer “are you real?” before they start. Fanvue's rules say a fully AI account must never tell fans it's a real person.

Do new chatters need my Fanvue password?

No. Add them as team members under a Fanvue manager account. They get their own login, and you keep yours.
